Radio is the channel that international planning decks treat as a rounding error and local buyers treat as the backbone. Both cannot be right.

In several of the markets we work in, radio delivers weekly reach figures that would justify a television budget, at a cost per thousand that is often an order of magnitude lower. It reaches people during the commute, during the working day, and in the languages purchase decisions are actually made in. And yet it routinely appears at the bottom of a channel split, below channels with a fraction of its reach.

## Why it gets under-planned

The reasons are mostly structural rather than strategic.

Radio measurement is patchier than digital measurement, so it looks less accountable in a deck even when it is delivering more. It is harder to produce centrally, because it needs local language and local voice. And it produces no screenshot, nobody puts a radio spot in an end-of-year showcase.

None of those are reasons it does not work. They are reasons it is inconvenient.

> The channel that is hardest to put in a case study is frequently the one carrying the campaign.

## Using it properly

Radio rewards a different creative discipline than film. The arguments that work are concrete and numerical, a price, a saving, a specific benefit stated plainly. Atmosphere does not survive the format.

It also rewards frequency over production value. A well-argued spot recorded simply and run often will outperform a beautifully produced one run occasionally. That is an uncomfortable trade for a creative team and an easy one for a finance director.

## The honest caveat

Radio is not a universal answer. In markets with high smartphone penetration and cheap data, its relative advantage narrows considerably, and for some categories and audiences digital audio has already taken the role. The point is not that radio always wins, it is that the planning process should have to argue its way _out_ of radio rather than never seriously considering it.
